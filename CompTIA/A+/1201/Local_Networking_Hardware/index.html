Network Types

A technician should be able to identify the scope and purpose of a network before selecting equipment or troubleshooting it.

Common Network Scopes

  • A PAN connects personal devices over a short range, such as Bluetooth accessories.
  • A LAN connects devices within one local area, while a WAN spans larger geographic distances using provider links.
  • You may also encounter specialized environments such as SANs for storage traffic or SOHO networks for small offices and home setups.

Logical Models

  • Client-server networks centralize services such as authentication, file sharing, and printing.
  • Peer-to-peer networks are simpler but less scalable and usually harder to manage securely.
  • Bandwidth, latency, and duplex settings all influence how well a network performs in practice.

Topologies and Use Cases

  • Star topology is the most common in Ethernet networks because each endpoint connects back to a central device such as a switch.
  • Mesh, bus, and ring concepts still appear in theory and in some specialty environments.
  • Knowing the type of network helps you predict where failures will spread and which device is most likely to be the single point of failure.
Summary

Scope, topology, and service model all shape how a network behaves. Good troubleshooting starts by knowing what kind of network you are dealing with.

A+ Exam Tip

If you see a question about small shared resources and centralized management, think client-server. If the focus is personal-range connectivity, think PAN and Bluetooth.

Networking Hardware

Modern local networks depend on a set of core devices that each have a distinct role. A+ expects you to match the device to the job.

Core Devices

  • A modem connects to an ISP handoff, while a router moves traffic between different networks.
  • A switch forwards frames inside a local network and is the most common center point in a wired LAN.
  • A wireless access point bridges Wi-Fi clients into the wired network, and a firewall enforces traffic policy.

Support Hardware

  • Patch panels, keystone jacks, wall plates, and structured cabling support clean installation and easier maintenance.
  • NICs, transceivers, and media converters help endpoints connect using the correct medium and speed.
  • SOHO devices often combine router, switch, firewall, and access point functions in one appliance.

Selection Clues

  • Choose hardware based on port count, speed, PoE support, wireless standard, and management features.
  • Enterprise switches and access points usually add VLAN support, remote administration, and stronger monitoring features.
  • Knowing where each device sits in the path makes troubleshooting much easier.
Summary

The job of the device matters more than the shape of the box. Routers route, switches switch, access points bridge wireless clients, and firewalls filter.

A+ Exam Tip

A+ commonly tests the difference between a switch and a router, and between a modem and the rest of the local equipment stack.

Network Cable Types

Cable choice affects speed, distance, interference resistance, and installation difficulty. Support technicians should know the common media and connectors on sight.

Twisted Pair Ethernet

  • Unshielded twisted pair is the standard medium for most office Ethernet runs and commonly ends in RJ45 connectors.
  • Categories such as Cat 5e, Cat 6, and Cat 6a define supported speeds and distance expectations.
  • T568A and T568B are the two main pinout standards; consistent termination matters more than which one is chosen.

Other Media

  • Coaxial cable still appears in cable broadband and some legacy environments.
  • Fiber optic cable supports long runs and high bandwidth and comes in single-mode and multimode varieties.
  • Telephone cabling uses RJ11 connectors and should not be confused with Ethernet connectors.

Install and Test

  • Patch cables connect devices to wall jacks or switches, while permanent links run through the structure of the building.
  • Cable testers confirm pinout, continuity, and some performance faults; certifiers go deeper for professional validation.
  • Watch for damage, poor bends, interference sources, and incorrect termination when diagnosing cable faults.
Summary

Media choice is about distance, bandwidth, and environment. The right connector and clean termination are just as important as the cable category.

A+ Exam Tip

For exam questions, separate connector type from cable type. RJ45 is the connector; Cat 6 is the twisted-pair cable standard.

Wireless Networking Types

Wireless support means more than memorizing Wi-Fi names. You need to recognize frequency bands, range tradeoffs, and nearby technologies such as Bluetooth and NFC.

Wi-Fi Standards

  • Common 802.11 generations include 802.11n, 802.11ac, and 802.11ax, with newer standards improving speed, efficiency, and device density.
  • 2.4 GHz generally offers more range but more interference, while 5 GHz offers more bandwidth and usually less congestion.
  • Some devices also support 6 GHz for newer Wi-Fi 6E or later deployments.

Short-Range Wireless

  • Bluetooth is widely used for peripherals, audio devices, and tethering over short ranges.
  • NFC enables tap-to-pair, proximity actions, and mobile payment scenarios.
  • Technologies such as RFID and cellular hotspot features may also appear in endpoint support questions.

Signal and Performance

  • Walls, interference, channel overlap, antenna placement, and client distance all affect signal quality.
  • SSID design, roaming behavior, and access point placement matter in multi-room environments.
  • A weak signal is not the only cause of poor Wi-Fi - congestion and misconfiguration can be just as important.
Summary

Wireless performance depends on standard, band, and environment. A strong troubleshooting habit is to separate coverage issues from interference or channel issues.

A+ Exam Tip

Remember the exam pattern: Bluetooth for personal-range peripherals, NFC for near-field tasks, and Wi-Fi for local network access.
