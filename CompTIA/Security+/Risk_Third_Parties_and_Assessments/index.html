Risk Management Processes and Concepts

Risk management identifies assets, threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, impact, and treatment choices in business context.

Core Ideas

  • Risk can be mitigated, transferred, avoided, or accepted depending on tolerance and cost.
  • Registers, owners, and review cycles keep risk visible after the first assessment.

Support Focus

  • Quantitative and qualitative reasoning may both appear in exam scenarios.
  • Residual risk remains even after controls are applied.
Summary

Risk management is about prioritizing action where it matters most.

Security+ Exam Tip

Do not confuse a vulnerability with the risk created by that vulnerability.

Vendor Management Concepts

Suppliers, partners, MSPs, and cloud providers expand capability but also expand the attack surface and shared obligations.

Core Ideas

  • Contracts, SLAs, due diligence, and access reviews help reduce third-party risk.
  • Remote support paths and service accounts create trust relationships that must be governed carefully.

Support Focus

  • Assess the provider before onboarding and monitor them afterward.
  • Third-party compromise can become your incident even if you never lost control directly.
Summary

Vendor risk is operational risk with an external dependency attached.

Security+ Exam Tip

Shared access and service providers are classic supply-chain pivots in Security+ questions.

Audits and Assessments

Organizations validate security through audits, compliance checks, control reviews, vulnerability assessments, and technical testing.

Core Ideas

  • An audit checks alignment to requirements, while an assessment may be broader or more exploratory.
  • Findings should lead to owners, dates, remediation plans, and verification.

Support Focus

  • Internal and external reviews serve different purposes but both matter.
  • Evidence quality determines how useful and defensible the outcome will be.
Summary

Assessments are only valuable when findings are turned into tracked improvement.

Security+ Exam Tip

An audit asks whether required controls are present and effective—not whether the organization feels secure.
